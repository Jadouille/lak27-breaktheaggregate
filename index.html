Summary

Learning analytics increasingly relies on complex data and models to understand and support learners. However, decisions about how data are collected, represented, analyzed, and modeled can introduce or obscure potential sources of bias. This half-day workshop will provide participants with practical techniques for interrogating educational data and assessing algorithmic fairness.

Through guided exploration of educational datasets and model outputs, participants will examine data distributions and representation, explore how analytical choices shape interpretations, disaggregate model performance across learner groups, and compare approaches to evaluating fairness. The workshop will combine short conceptual introductions with individual and small-group activities and facilitated discussion. Participants will consider how questions of bias and fairness arise throughout the learning analytics pipeline and leave with practical approaches they can apply to their own practice.

Goals and Intended Outcomes

This workshop aims to provide participants with practical approaches for interrogating the complexity and fairness of data used in learning analytics. Through a series of hands-on activities, participants will move from exploring and understanding educational data to examining how analytical and modeling choices can shape conclusions about learners. The workshop will encourage participants to consider fairness throughout the analytic process and develop techniques they can apply within their own research and practice. Specifically, the primary goals of this workshop are:

  1. A multi-disciplinary roadmap to fairness in research in learning analytics. Provide the tools to conduct fairness analysis to a wide audience, from novices to experts. We start with simple methods, such as stratified analyses, to help participants disambiguate the different signals present in their data. We then continue with more advanced methods that address current challenges in fairness research, such as small-N problems, which can arise when analyzing intersectional groups or working with very sensitive data in local institutions.
  2. A place to re-assess previous research and current datasets. Provide participants with a recent and relevant educational dataset to uncover the stories usually hidden by traditional learning analytics practices, and collectively decide how these analyses should best be integrated into current research pipelines.
  3. A venue to discuss the consequences of one-size-fits-all models. Discuss the consequences one-size-fits-all approaches can have for specific demographic groups when deployed in diverse institutions worldwide. Understand who benefits, who gets disadvantaged, and to what extent, by analyzing recent work conducted in the LAK community.
  4. Developing a common vision. A co-constructed set of community guidelines to help the LAK community strengthen fairness considerations in learning analytics research and practice.

Background

The data that informs learning analytics is continually expanding, as are the processes we use to mine and understand it. These data can include assessment outcomes, demographic information, self-report measures, interaction logs, and increasingly complex behavioral and multimodal traces. Although these sources create opportunities to represent learning processes at greater depth, they also introduce analytical challenges. Educational data does not neutrally represent learners. Decisions about which constructs to measure, how to operationalize them, when and from whom to collect data, and at what level to aggregate data shape the conclusions that can be drawn.

These challenges matter because similar patterns in observed data may reflect substantially different underlying learning processes. For example, similar behavioral traces may reflect different cognitive or motivational conditions, meaning interventions based on those traces may have differing effects. Conversely, aggregated measures may obscure meaningful within-learner variation over time. Learning analytics must therefore consider measurement choices, granularity, and the relationship between observable behavior and the constructs researchers seek to understand.

These complexities have direct implications for algorithmic fairness. Bias in educational algorithms can emerge at multiple points in the analytic pipeline, including through decisions about measurement and data collection as well as model development and deployment. Within learning analytics, this has motivated growing attention to the relationship between algorithmic fairness, the absence of bias, and broader questions of educational equity. Importantly, fairness cannot necessarily be established through a single technical criterion. The interpretation of fairness metrics depends on the underlying data-generating process, intended application, and assumptions about what constitutes fair treatment, while an analytic system’s consequences depend on the broader sociotechnical context in which it is deployed.

Despite growing attention to fairness and responsible AI, researchers and practitioners often face a gap between recognizing these concerns conceptually and having practical methods for investigating them in their own work. Questions such as these can be investigated using accessible exploratory, visualization, disaggregation, and model-evaluation techniques:

  • Who is represented in this dataset?
  • What patterns are obscured by aggregation?
  • How does model performance vary across groups?
  • What are the consequences of different kinds of errors?

This workshop addresses this gap through a hands-on exploration of educational data and algorithmic fairness. Rather than treating fairness as a single metric or algorithmic bias as solely a property of a trained model, participants will work through the analytical pipeline, from understanding and visualizing data through disaggregating model performance and examining the consequences of modeling decisions. In doing so, the workshop aims to develop participants’ practical capacity to critically interrogate the data and algorithms that increasingly underpin learning analytics research and practice.

Program

This half-day session is structured as a Guided Exploration Workshop, emphasizing active, hands-on participation rather than presentations. Short facilitator-led introductions provide the conceptual and technical grounding for each activity, followed by hands-on analysis, small-group interpretation, and whole-group discussion. Activities follow a progressive structure, with each stage introducing additional complexity into participants’ analysis.

DurationSession
~20 min Introduction
Learning objectives and schedule of the workshop. The organizers introduce themselves and ask participants about their personal objectives for the workshop.
~50 min Plenary + collective discussions
An accessible introduction to fairness methods in learning analytics for a mixed audience, touching on disaggregated performance, how different error metrics can emphasize different analytical and societal flaws, and how to address low-data contexts in intersectional analyses. Researchers new to these techniques will get a grasp of what is being done in the field, and experts can debate which techniques are most relevant to their own research.
~50 min Hands-on exercise & group work
Using their own dataset or the provided recent educational dataset, participants compare the different stories they can extract from their data based on aggregation style, choice of error metric, model calibration, and statistical analyses.
— Break
~50 min From model errors to real-world consequences
Case discussions on the real-world consequences of biased analyses, drawing on both the hands-on exercise and current research being conducted at LAK. Guiding questions:
  • Who experiences the false positives, and who experiences the false negatives? Do both bring similar positive/negative consequences?
  • How do consequences vary by institution, context, and intervention type?
  • What happens when a prediction becomes an action?
  • How do feedback loops form?
~30 min A fairness manifesto for the LAK community
Participants collaboratively draft a set of guiding principles and recommendations for the LAK community, centered on the importance of systematically integrating fairness practices into learning analytics research.

Call for Participation

We welcome researchers, practitioners, and students interested in bias and fairness in learning analytics, from those new to fairness analysis to experienced researchers. No prior experience with fairness methods is required: the workshop starts with simple methods and builds toward more advanced techniques.

  • We anticipate 10–20 participants.
  • The workshop will be conducted in English.
  • Participants are encouraged to bring their own dataset to analyze during the hands-on session. A recent and relevant educational dataset will also be provided for those who do not bring one.
To express interest or ask questions, please contact the organizers by email (fstinar2@illinois.edu). Registration details will follow the LAK27 conference registration process.
